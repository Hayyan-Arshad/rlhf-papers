Learning a reward function from human preferences.

The paper in one diagram

Three asynchronous processes exchange trajectories, human comparisons, and reward-model parameters.

PROCESS 1

Policy (\pi) interacts

The policy produces trajectories (\tau^1,\ldots,\tau^n) in the environment.

\(\tau=(o_0,a_0),\ldots,(o_T,a_T)\)
→
PROCESS 2

Human compares

Segments (\sigma^1,\sigma^2) are shown as short clips. The label can be prefer 1, prefer 2, tie, or incomparable.

\(D=\{(\sigma^1,\sigma^2,\mu)\}\)
→
PROCESS 3

Reward model (\hat r_\theta)

Supervised learning fits a predictor whose summed reward explains the preference data.

\(\hat R_\theta(\sigma)=\sum_t\hat r_\theta(o_t,a_t)\)
The learned reward returns to the policy process: \(\pi\) is optimized to maximize \(\sum_t \hat r_\theta(o_t,a_t)\), then new trajectory clips are sampled.

What the implementation adds

These details are easy to miss in a game, but they are central to the paper’s scientific contribution.

Soft labels

A human label is a distribution (\mu\), allowing ties and uncertainty instead of forcing every pair into a binary choice.

Reward ensemble

The method trains multiple predictors, normalizes them independently, and averages their predictions.

Human noise

The paper models a 10% chance that a rater responds uniformly at random.

True RL update

Atari uses A2C; simulated robotics uses TRPO. The learned reward is normalized before policy optimization.

This explorer is a faithful method visualization, not a reproduction of the authors’ neural networks or training compute. The paper reports seven Atari environments, with real feedback collected over hundreds to thousands of 1–2 second comparisons, and novel behaviors such as robotic backflips and one-legged locomotion.
